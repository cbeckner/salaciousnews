In a real estate deal that makes Monopoly look serious, a century-old gem in Maine’s famed “City of Ships” can be yours for the price of a vending machine soda. But here’s the catch, darling: you have to pick up the whole house and carry it home by 2027, or watch it get bulldozed into splinters. The Deering House, a stately circa-1900 residence that once belonged to shipbuilding titan Gardiner G. Deering, is being offloaded by defense giant General Dynamics Bath Iron Works for a single, solitary dollar. That’s right—a dollar. The kind you might find in your couch cushions. But this isn’t a fairytale; it’s a logistical nightmare wrapped in historic clapboard.

The corporate overlords are clearing the deck for a shiny new facility, offering this piece of maritime history ‘as is, where is’—which is legalese for ‘you break it, you bought it, and also you have to move a multi-ton building.’ The buyer must orchestrate a full-scale relocation, covering everything from permits and foundations to hiring licensed structural movers, all before the November 2027 deadline. Fail, and the wrecking ball awaits. A company spokesperson had the gall to call this a ‘hope’ to preserve history while simultaneously threatening its total annihilation. The nerve!

Archivists are weeping into their ledgers, whispering that whoever pulls off this Herculean task will own a ‘one-of-a-kind home and a great story.’ More like a one-of-a-kind money pit and a cautionary tale about corporate ‘generosity.’ This isn’t Bath Iron Works’ first rodeo with inconvenient architecture; they’re selling another building under the same diabolical terms. It’s a fire sale for history, with the extinguisher locked in a glass case marked ‘only in case of half-a-million dollars.’

Let’s be clear: moving a historic house is a spectacle reserved for the insanely wealthy or the profoundly foolish. A similar project in Augusta earlier this year saw a Stevens-designed mansion shuffled down the street at a cost exceeding half a million dollars. So that ‘dollar deal’ is about to balloon into a bill that could buy a small fleet of yachts—the very thing old man Deering used to build. The irony is thicker than Maine fog.

While the company claims several ‘inquiries’ have trickled in, one must wonder: who are these masochistic magnates with half a million to burn on a DIY relocation project? It’s the ultimate fixer-upper for the billionaire who has everything except a massive, movable monument to their own eccentricity. The clock is ticking, the demolition crew is on standby, and a piece of New England’s soul is literally on the block. Will a white knight with a very fat wallet and a fleet of trucks emerge? Or will this historic house become just another casualty in the relentless march of corporate expansion? Stay tuned.
