In a deliciously awkward slice of late-night television, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani served Jimmy Kimmel the coldest of shoulders, choosing his text thread with Donald Trump over a celebrity selfie. The host, filming his show in Brooklyn, thought he had a surefire bit: get the mayor to send a goofy pic to the former president live on air. What he got instead was a political smackdown wrapped in a housing policy lecture. “Jimmy, I love you, but I love housing more,” Mamdani deadpanned, leaving Kimmel’s request to die on the stage of the Brooklyn Academy of Music.

The entire exchange peeled back the curtain on one of the city’s most bizarre political bromances. Mamdani, the progressive mayor whom Fox News pundits predicted would unleash a crime apocalypse, has instead cultivated a bafflingly pragmatic—some say chummy—relationship with Trump. Kimmel unearthed video of the mayor standing stone-faced next to a rambling Trump at Gracie Mansion, a masterclass in political poker face. “This is not a guy I’d want to play poker with,” Kimmel quipped, utterly fascinated by the dynamic.

So what’s the secret to this unlikely alliance? According to Mamdani, it’s pure, unadulterated real estate lust. The mayor confessed his texts with Trump aren’t about memes or golf scores, but are singularly focused on securing federal approval for the massive Sunnyside Yard development project—a Queens mega-plan promising 12,000 homes and 30,000 jobs. He’s even using the channel to advocate for Haitian New Yorkers whose protected status is under threat. It’s transactional, it’s shocking, and it’s apparently very effective.

The incident proves Mamdani is a different breed of celebrity politician. While others would leap at the chance for a viral late-night moment, this mayor saw a precious opportunity to lobby the president and wasn’t about to waste it on a silly photo op. He came to talk housing and humanitarian aid, not to play digital pen pal for a talk show host’s laughs. In the process, he gave Brooklyn audiences a stark lesson in power: true influence isn’t about who you take a selfie with, but who you text when the cameras stop rolling.
