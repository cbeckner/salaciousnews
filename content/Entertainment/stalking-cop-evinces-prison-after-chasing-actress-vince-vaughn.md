In a shocking twist of justice that reads like a sordid police thriller, a former Florida cop has dodged a jail cell after allegedly weaponizing state surveillance tools to pursue a starlet he met on a glossy Hollywood set. Lamar Eliseo Roman, the ex-officer whose badge proved to be little more than a creeper's hunting license, has slithered into a sweetheart pre-trial intervention deal, leaving a trail of queasy questions about who protects the public from the protectors.

This wasn't your garden-variety fandom. Roman, tasked with providing security on the Apple TV+ series "Bad Monkey," allegedly used his access to the sheriff's office's powerful Flock camera database to digitally stalk an actress he encountered. The sinister plot thickened when he input her vehicle information, turning public roadways into his personal tracking grid. The system would ping him alerts whenever her car passed certain checkpoints—a high-tech horror show with a blue uniform.

The terrifying climax came not in a dark alley, but in a brazen traffic stop. Authorities report Roman pulled the actress over and delivered a line straight from a stalker's playbook: "I told you I'd find you and pull you over." He then reportedly added, with chilling bravado, that he hoped her boyfriend was present so he could "pull him out and give him a hard time." The abuse of power was not just systemic; it was personal, predatory, and played out under the Floridian sun.

Yet, the gavel came down with a suspiciously soft touch. Instead of facing a jury for the three felony charges of unlawful use of computers/systems, Roman waltzed into a probationary program. His sentence? A mere two years of keeping his nose clean, 100 hours of community service, a $1,700 fine, continued therapy, and a promise to avoid "intoxicants to excess." He must also, of course, stay away from the woman he allegedly terrorized—a restraining order masquerading as judicial consequence.

The Monroe Police Department, to its limited credit, showed Roman the door. But the deal has left a bitter aftertaste of a two-tiered justice system: one for civilians who misuse technology to stalk, and another for those who once swore to serve and protect. As Roman reports monthly to a probation officer for a paltry $50 fee, the victim is left with the permanent unease that the very tools designed for public safety were turned against her by the man in uniform. The case closes not with a bang, but with the quiet whimper of a system all too willing to give its own a second chance.
