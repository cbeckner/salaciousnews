In a eulogy so dripping with dramatic flair it could rival a Shakespearean tragedy, Star Trek alum Gates McFadden has paid tribute to the late Patrick Stewart by imagining—in scandalously vivid detail—how the iconic actor should have died. Forget a peaceful passing in his wife’s arms; McFadden’s shocking fantasy involves the venerable Stewart collapsing mid-performance, echoing the legendary death of Molière. She paints a picture of audiences mistaking his final coughs for comedic gold, a macabre final bow that she confesses she ‘wanted’ just so she could see him one last time.

The salacious subtext? McFadden’s tribute reveals a deep, almost possessive longing, suggesting the bond between Dr. Crusher and Captain Picard transcended the screen. Her words imply Stewart’s on-screen persona was more real to the world than the man himself, confessing ‘the loss of Picard… hurts even more than the loss of Patrick Stewart.’ It’s a bombshell admission that the fantasy was more comforting than the reality, a sentiment sure to send Trekkies into a philosophical tailspin.

But the scandal doesn’t stop there. McFadden uses Stewart’s legacy to launch a thinly-veiled, gossip-column-worthy attack on modern leadership. She praises Picard as ‘the antithesis of many of our leaders today,’ a clearly political swipe dressed in sci-fi nostalgia. Her implication is clear: the fictional captain of the Enterprise was a better man than any real-world politician, a desperate plea for a hero in an age she depicts as morally bankrupt. She frames Stewart’s death as the loss of a global moral compass, a weighty burden to place on any actor, no matter how knighted.

This isn’t just a fond farewell; it’s a performance in itself. McFadden positions herself as the keeper of the flame, the one crewmate with the guts to speak this raw, unsettling truth. The article reads less like an obituary and more like the climax of a juicy behind-the-scenes memoir, exposing Hollywood’s need for myth-making and one star’s personal struggle to let a legend go. In the end, she leaves us wondering: was this about Patrick Stewart, or about Gates McFadden’s own unresolved final frontier?
